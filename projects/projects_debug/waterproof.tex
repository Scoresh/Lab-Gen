\subsubsection{Manufactoring Questions}\label{manufactoring-questions}

A manufacturer of boots plans to conduct an experiment to compare a new method of waterproofing to the current method. The appearance of the boots is not changed by either method. One hundred people who live in Seattle, where it rains frequently, volunteer to participate in the study. They will wear the boots as they normally would for 6 months. At the end of the 6 months, the boots will be returned to the company to be evaluated for water damage.

\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  Identify the following:
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  The explanatory variable.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Old versus. New Waterproofing Method
  \end{itemize}
\item
  The response variable.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Difference in Water Damage
  \end{itemize}
\item
  The experimental units.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Subjects: People who live in Seattle participating in the study
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\tightlist
\item
  Identify some possibly confounding variables.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Possible confounding variables include:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Different human weights
  \item
    Different levels of rainfall
  \item
    Time spent in the rain
  \item
    Tragedy of the Commons may lead to people abusing the free shoe.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}
\tightlist
\item
  Describe a design for this experiment that uses the 100 volunteers. Include commentary on how it would be implemented. Recall, there are three experimental designs in the Great Bay Stat curriculum:
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Completely randomized design

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    To implement "Completely Randomized Design," I would assign a unique identification to each volunteer. Then, I would randomly choose 50 volunteers to wear the experimental boot, and other 50 volunteers to wear the control boot. This way, each person would have the ability to wear the boot for 6 months, and they would send it back to evaluate for water damage. Each volunteer would be instructed to wear the boot as if they would a regular boot.\\
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Randomized block design

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    To ensure that there are homogenous groups (blocks) selected with this experiment, I would make groups of the 100 volunteers based on age-groups. I would split the volunteers into Under 20, 20-65, and 65+. This way, each homogenous group will have different times wearing the shoe; volunteers under 20 and people ages 65+ are likely to spend a lot of time outdoors, while people between the ages of 20-65 are likely to be preoccupied with their job. Then, I would split the group in half; 50\% would wear the experimental group, and the rest would wear the control group. This way, each person would have the ability to wear the boot for 6 months, and they would send it back to evaluate for water damage. Each volunteer would be instructed to wear the boot as if they would a regular boot.\\
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Matched pairs design

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    In this case, I could match volunteers most closely based on age, gender, and weight. This would then allow me to assign one of the two with the experimental boot, and the other with the control group. This way, each person would have the ability to wear the boot for 6 months, and they would send it back to evaluate for water damage. Each volunteer would be instructed to wear the boot as if they would a regular boot.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

I like the "Randomized Block Design" most in this case due to its ability to split up the volunteer group into homogenous subgroups.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{3}
\tightlist
\item
  Three of the four principles of experimental design are necessary for any well-designed experiment: control, randomization, replication. For the design that you chose in part charlie, describe the why and the how you would implement each of the three principles.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  I chose "Randomized Block Design" because it exemplifies three out of the four principles of experimental design. The "RBD" experiment has a control group; half of the participants wear a regular boot to express causation in the results. Randomization is also key; this experiment is randomized each time such that each combination has an equal chance of occuring. Furthermore, this experiment is also able to be replicated with relatively the same results.
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{4}
\tightlist
\item
  Could your design be double blinded? Explain.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Yes; I would just not tell the person if the boot is the control group or the experimental group, removing the placebo bias. People do not if they are receiving the experimental group or the control group, so they are likely to wear the boot relatively the same.
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{5}
\tightlist
\item
  Could you generalize the results of this study to customers who purchase these boots from across the U.S.? Explain.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  No; these are 100 participants from Seattle. Seattle has a particular climate; and the experimental design yields results specific to Seattle. However, if I were to expand this design to various places around the United States and yield positive results, I could generalize this to the US.
\end{itemize}
